# Writing anti-patterns for [Company]

[Language note: this guide is written for English. If the company publishes in other languages,
say here which, and that the rhythm checks apply to them while the word lists do not.]

## Why this guide exists

Readers have become good at spotting writing produced on autopilot, whether by a person in a hurry
or by an AI model. Once they spot it they skim, and they trust the rest of the piece less. For a
young company that is expensive: its writing is often the first evidence a reader has of how the
company thinks.

When you find one of these habits in a draft, do not just delete it. It usually marks a spot where
the writer had nothing specific to say. Find the specific thing (the number, the name, the
example, the actual reason) and write that, or cut the sentence.

Judge each case from the reader's side: does this sentence tell them something they did not know a
line earlier? A habit that passes that test can stay. Most of the time the sentence carrying it
fails, and the fix is new material rather than new wording.

## Rhythm and structure

### Closing reversals
Ending a paragraph or a piece on a turn: "It's not about the software. It's about the people."
"The answer isn't more meetings. It's fewer." It promises an insight and delivers a slogan, and
readers now see it coming. End on the most useful specific point, or stop when the point is made.

### Everything in threes
Three adjectives, three benefits, three parallel clauses, by default. Real material rarely comes
in threes. Count what you actually have. Two good reasons are stronger than two good reasons and
a filler.

### Sentences set apart for effect
A short sentence given a paragraph of its own so that it lands harder: "Then everything changed."
The white space asks the reader to stop and take note, and most of these sentences do not repay
the stop. Put the sentence back into the paragraph it belongs to unless what it reports really is
the turn the whole piece depends on.

### Paragraphs all the same length
Every paragraph three or four sentences, built the same way: claim, explanation, example, wrap.
Let a paragraph be as long as its idea. Some ideas take one sentence; some take eight.

### Dash-heavy rhythm
Asides and reveals hung off long dashes in sentence after sentence. A dash now and then is fine.
Most of the time a comma, a colon, brackets or a full stop does the job and reads more calmly.

### Set-ups and reveals
"Here's the thing:", "The result?", "The best part?", a rhetorical question answered in the next
sentence. They delay the point to create drama the content has not earned. State the point.

### Matching pairs
"Faster for your team. Simpler for your customers." Short, symmetrical sentences in pairs or
runs sound like an advert. Vary sentence length with the thought.

### Lists where prose belongs
Breaking an argument into bullets, each with a bold lead-in, removes the connections between the
points, which are usually the argument. Use lists for things that really are lists: steps,
options, specifications.

## Words

### Throat-clearing
Openers that delay the point: "In today's fast-moving world", "When it comes to", "It's worth
noting that", "Let's dive in", "In this post we'll explore". Start with the point. The first
sentence should say something a reader could disagree with or act on.

### Abstract nouns doing the work of verbs
"Drive alignment", "enable transformation", "deliver value", "unlock potential". They hide who
does what. Name the person, the action and the result: "the finance team closes the month in two
days instead of six".

### Stock metaphors and hype words
Landscape, journey, ecosystem, game-changer, seamless, robust, cutting-edge, leverage, navigate,
empower, elevate, revolutionise, supercharge, unlock, delve, tapestry, testament. They are so
common that they carry no meaning. Use the plain word, or the specific one.

### Sincerity markers
"Honestly,", "To be clear,", "Let's be real", "The truth is". They are padding that signals
effort: the sentence after them is no more true or more important for having them. Cut the marker
and check that the sentence still says something.

### Intensifiers and stacked hedges
"Incredibly", "truly", "genuinely", "really", and hedges piled up: "this may potentially help to
somewhat reduce". Make the claim at the strength the evidence supports, once.

### Summaries of what was just said
"In short", "Ultimately", "At the end of the day", followed by the paragraph again. If the piece
is clear, the reader does not need it repeated. If it is not, fix the piece.

## Stance

### Claims without a subject
"Many teams struggle with onboarding." Which teams, how many, according to whom? Give the source
or the number, or say it as your own observation.

### Manufactured enthusiasm
"We're thrilled to announce", "Great question", "We're so excited". Readers discount it. Say what
happened and why it matters to them.

### Balance as a reflex
"While both approaches have merit, it depends on your needs." Sometimes true, often a way of not
taking a view. If the company has a view, state it and give the reason.

## Listening pass

Run this on every draft before anyone else sees it.

1. Read it aloud at speaking pace. Mark every place you stumble, run out of breath, or sound like
   an advert.
2. Read the first sentence. Does it say something? If not, delete openers until it does.
3. Read the last sentence of each paragraph. Is it a reversal, a slogan or a summary? Rewrite it or
   cut it.
4. Count the lists of three. Keep only the ones where there really are three things.
5. Look at the shape on the page. Are the paragraphs all the same length? Are there several
   one-line paragraphs?
6. Count the dashes. More than one in a paragraph is usually too many.
7. Check every claim for a subject and a source. Who? How many? Says who?
8. Search for the words in the lists above and in the messaging framework's "avoid" list.
9. Apply the swap test: put a competitor's name in place of [Company]. If a sentence is still
   true, make it specific or cut it.
10. Check "What we can say where" in the messaging framework. Is any number here meant to stay
    private?
11. If the piece is written for a named person, read their voice note. Would they say this out
    loud, in these words?

## Worked example (not copied into the brand folder)

A fictional company, Larkfield Cargo, rents electric cargo bikes to small delivery businesses.
A first draft of a paragraph for its website:

> In today's fast-moving delivery landscape, small businesses face mounting pressure. They need
> solutions that are affordable, reliable and sustainable. That's where we come in. Larkfield
> isn't just a bike rental company. It's a partner in your journey to greener deliveries.

What is wrong with it: a throat-clearing opener, "landscape" and "journey", an automatic list of
three adjectives, a one-line reveal ("That's where we come in"), a closing reversal, and no fact a
reader could check. The swap test fails on every sentence.

The rewrite, after asking for specifics:

> A Larkfield bike carries up to 120 kg and costs a florist or a bakery less per month than the
> fuel for a small van. We deliver it, service it every six weeks and swap it the same day if
> anything breaks, so you never lose a morning's deliveries.

It names the customers, gives two checkable numbers and says what the company actually does. It
also needed information the first draft did not have, which is the usual result of this check.
