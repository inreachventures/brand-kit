# Stage 1: Research

The research shows what the category already sounds and looks like, so the founder can choose
deliberately where to fit in and where to stand apart. Its most useful outputs are the list of
category clichés, which every later stage avoids, and the gaps, which feed the positioning
options.

## Choose the competitors

Aim for four to eight. Start from the names in the intake notes and add a mix:

- **Direct competitors**: same product, same customers.
- **Adjacent players**: a different product the same customer could buy to solve the same
  problem.
- **The status quo**: what customers do instead (a spreadsheet, an agency, hiring someone, doing
  nothing). Describe how it is talked about, even though it has no website.
- **A brand the founder admires**, if they named one, even from another category. It shows what
  they are drawn to.

Show the founder the list and the reason for each before you start, and let them change it.

## What to capture for each

Look at the homepage, a product page, the about page and one or two recent posts or social
profiles. For each competitor capture:

- **Positioning line**: their main headline, quoted exactly.
- **Audience**: who the copy addresses, and who it seems to ignore.
- **Proof they lead with**: numbers, logos, awards, testimonials.
- **Tone**: a few observations, each with a short quoted example (formal or casual, long or short
  sentences, jargon, humour, how much they talk about themselves).
- **Colours**: the main colours, as hex values where the page's styles show them, otherwise
  described.
- **Type and imagery**: serif or sans, weight, photography or illustration, the style of either.
- **Words they repeat**: the terms and claims that keep coming back.

Record the address and the date for every page you used.

## Find the patterns

Once all competitors are captured, step back and look across them.

**Category clichés.** List what most of them do: the words and claims ("seamless", "all-in-one",
"powered by AI", "trusted by thousands"), the colours, the imagery, the tone. A cliché is anything
used by more than half the set, or so common that a reader would not notice it. This list becomes
a "words to avoid" and "colours to avoid" input for stages 3 and 4. Name the clichés specifically
for this category.

**Gaps.** Look for what nobody says or does:

- an audience every competitor ignores or talks down to
- a claim nobody makes, or nobody can prove
- a tone nobody uses (plain where everyone is breathless, warm where everyone is cold)
- a colour space or visual style nobody occupies

Not every gap is worth filling. Some exist because nobody wants them. Note each one and leave the
judgement to stage 2.

**Implications.** Write a few observations stage 2 should test, phrased as questions or
possibilities, not decisions.

## Without web access

If you cannot search or fetch pages in this runtime:

1. Say so to the founder plainly before you start, and explain that the analysis will be limited
   to what they can give you.
2. Ask them to paste each competitor's homepage headline and a paragraph of copy, and to share
   screenshots if they can. Screenshots let you note colours and type.
3. You may add what you already know about well-known companies, marked as background knowledge
   that may be out of date. Never invent a quote, a colour or a claim.
4. Without competitor copy you cannot count how many in the set use a phrase, so widen what
   counts as evidence for the clichés: the founder's own existing copy (it usually borrows the
   category's language), their descriptions of how competitors talk, and the words customers
   repeat back from competitors. Give each cliché its source, for example "our old homepage",
   "Maya's description of two competitors" or "pasted copy from [competitor]", instead of a count.
5. Put the limitation at the top of the document, so later readers know the evidence is thinner.

## Present and confirm

Before writing the file, give the founder a short summary in chat: the clichés and the gaps. Ask
whether it matches what they see in the market and what you missed. Their reactions often say a
lot about the positioning they want.

## Template: `brand/research/competitor-analysis.md`

```
# Competitor analysis

[Date. Sources used: web research, founder input, or both. If the research was limited, say how
and why here.]

## Scope
[The competitors chosen, with one line each on why they are in the set and what type they are:
direct, adjacent, status quo, admired.]

## Competitors

### [Competitor name]
- **Website:** [address, date checked]
- **Positioning line:** ["their headline, quoted exactly"]
- **Audience:** [who the copy addresses, who it ignores]
- **Proof they lead with:** [numbers, logos, awards, testimonials]
- **Tone:** [observations, each with a short quoted example]
- **Colours:** [hex values or descriptions]
- **Type and imagery:** [what you saw]
- **Words they repeat:** [list]

[Repeat for each competitor.]

## At a glance

| Competitor | Positioning in a few words | Main audience | Tone | Main colours |
|------------|----------------------------|---------------|------|--------------|
| [name] | [summary] | [audience] | [tone] | [colours] |

## Category clichés

### Words and claims
[The overused words and claims, with how many competitors use each, or, without web access, the
source of each one.]

### Colours and imagery
[The overused colours, with hex values where known, and the imagery styles everyone uses.]

### Tone
[The default tone of the category.]

## Gaps
[Each gap: what it is, the evidence for it, and a guess at why nobody occupies it.]

## For stage 2 to test
[Observations phrased as questions or possibilities, not decisions.]

## Limits of this analysis
[What was not checked, and anything taken on the founder's word or from background knowledge.]
```

End the stage as SKILL.md describes. Stage 2 builds positioning options on these gaps.
