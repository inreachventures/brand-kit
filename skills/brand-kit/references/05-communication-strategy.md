# Stage 5: Communication strategy

The communication strategy says where the company will talk, how often, about what, and who
signs it off. It is a plan the team will actually follow, which for a small company means a short
one. A plan for five channels that is abandoned in a month does more harm than a plan for one
channel that runs for a year: an empty blog or a feed that stopped in spring tells visitors the
company stalled.

Read the positioning, the audience files, the messaging framework (especially channel
calibration), the tone of voice, and from the intake notes the milestones ahead, the people who
will publish, and the channels they already use.

## The interview

One cluster at a time, as always.

**Goals.** What should communication achieve in the next six to twelve months? Tie it to the
milestones from the intake: customers for a launch, credibility before a fundraise, applicants for
key hires. Ask which goal comes first if they conflict.

**Channels.** Where do the audiences actually look? Which channels does the company use today,
and which of those are working? Which does each person enjoy using? Owned channels are the ones
the company controls: the website and blog, a newsletter, its social accounts, the founders'
personal accounts, community spaces it runs. Press, partners and paid campaigns sit outside this
plan unless the founder wants them noted.

**Time.** How many hours a week can each person give, in an ordinary week? Ask for the honest
number, not the hoped-for one.

**Approvals and limits.** Who has to see what before it goes out? What must never be said in
public? Are there regulatory or contractual limits, or customers who have not agreed to be named?

**Success.** What would make them say in six months that this was worth it?

## Keeping it sustainable

Steer the plan towards what a small team can keep up:

- **Fewer channels, done properly.** One or two owned channels where the audience already is,
  plus the website. Add more only after the first ones have run for a few months.
- **A committed minimum, not an ambition.** For each channel and each person, set the cadence
  they would still hit in a bad month. Anything above it is a bonus. A plan that needs a good
  month every month will fail in the first busy one.
- **Topics from real work.** The best material comes from what the company already does:
  questions customers ask, objections in sales calls, product changes, numbers the company can
  share, things the founders disagree with. A topic bank fed from these never runs dry.
- **Write once, use several times.** A long piece becomes a few posts, a newsletter section and
  a paragraph for sales emails.
- **Approval that does not block.** Agree which content needs sign-off and which does not, with a
  turnaround time. A post that waits a week for approval is usually dead.
- **Review after ninety days.** Keep what works, drop what does not, and adjust the minimums to
  what actually happened.

Push back gently if the founder commits to more than their hours allow. Show the arithmetic.
