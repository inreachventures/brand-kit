# Tone of voice

Brindlewick sounds like a mechanic talking to another mechanic across the counter: plain, exact
about the work, warm without being matey. In Maya's words, "warm, not twee."

## Principles

### Name the part, not the category
Say which bike, which part, which job, how many minutes. A shop owner trusts a sentence that
could only have been written by someone who has done the work, and skims one that could be about
any software. Use the trade's own words, and only the ones a shop owner uses; no engineering
jargon for show.

**Like this:** "Distributors name the same brake pad four different ways, so we match on the
manufacturer part number first and the name last."

**Not like this:** "Our intelligent parts engine leverages advanced matching to streamline
procurement across suppliers."

### Plain, but warm
Short words and short sentences, but written by a person, to a person. Contractions are fine. No
exclamation marks, no "thrilled", and no forced jokes. Warmth comes from noticing what the
reader's day is like, not from adjectives.

**Like this:** "You're set up. Customers can book repairs with you online from today, at the
link below."

**Not like this:** "Welcome aboard! We're thrilled to have you on this exciting journey with us."

### Start at the bench
Open with a bike, a customer or a moment in the shop, not with Brindlewick. The reader should
recognise their own day before they hear about the product. Keep it to a line or two; this is a
way in, not a story for its own sake.

**Like this:** "The phone rings with your hands in a bottom bracket. With Brindlewick, that
customer books online and gets a text when the bike's ready."

**Not like this:** "Brindlewick is a comprehensive platform that helps workshops manage bookings,
communications and inventory."

### Confident, never loud
Say a thing once, at the strength the evidence allows. Give the number when there is one (140
shops) and say nothing when there is not. No superlatives, no "best", no hype words. A claim we
cannot prove does not go in.

**Like this:** "140 shops in the UK and Ireland use Brindlewick."

**Not like this:** "Trusted by hundreds of shops, Brindlewick is the UK's leading workshop
software!"

### Explain the why, in a sentence
When the product does something a particular way, say why in one plain sentence, the way Tom
does. Shop owners respect a reason; engineers we want to hire look for one. Stop after the reason.

**Like this:** "The customer gets a text when the bike's ready, because 'is it ready?' is a call
nobody should take with their hands full."

**Not like this:** "Our notification system has been carefully designed with the customer
experience in mind to ensure optimal engagement."

## We sound like / we do not sound like

| We sound like | We do not sound like |
|---------------|----------------------|
| A mechanic at the counter | A software salesperson |
| The note on the job card: what was done, what is next | A brochure |
| Someone who has been there at nine at night | Someone who has read about it |
| Warm | Twee or matey |
| Sure of the facts we have | Excited |

## By channel

| Channel | How the voice adapts | Length and format |
|---------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Website | Most plain. Lead with what the shop gets and the founder's history; one bench moment per page at most. | Headlines under ten words. Short paragraphs. |
| LinkedIn (Maya) | Most personal. A specific repair or customer, first person. | Three to six short sentences. No hashtags beyond one or two. |
| LinkedIn (Tom) | Careful and explanatory. One problem, how it was solved, why. | Two to five paragraphs. |
| Instagram | Shop stories: the shop, the mechanic, the bike, in their words where they agree. | One to three sentences under a photo. Name the shop only with permission. |
| Monthly email to shops | What changed in the product, one tip, one story from a shop. | Under 300 words. One subject per section. |
| Sales messages | Start with their shop, not with us. One question, one fact. | Under 120 words. |
| Support replies | Answer first, then the why. Never blame the shop. | As short as the answer allows. |
| Investor updates | Plainest of all. Numbers, what happened, what is next. | Bullet points are fine here. |
| Job adverts | What the work is and who the shops are. Riding helps; say so. | Under 400 words. No "rockstars", no "fast-paced". |

## Before and after

### Homepage headline (brindlewick.example)
**Before:** "The all-in-one seamless solution for modern bike shops."

**After:** "Bookings, quotes, ready texts and parts orders for independent bike shops. Made by a
mechanic."

**What changed:** "All-in-one" and "seamless" are the category's two clichés; the rewrite lists
what the software does instead. "Modern" became "independent", which is who the shops are.

### Signup email, opening line
**Before:** "Welcome aboard! We're thrilled to have you on this exciting journey with us."

**After:** "You're set up. Customers can book repairs with you online from today, at the link
below."

**What changed:** The new customer wants to know what happens next, not how we feel. The rewrite
tells them, and drops the exclamation marks and "journey".

### Sales one-liner
**Before:** "Brindlewick leverages cutting-edge technology to revolutionise workshop management."

**After:** "Brindlewick takes bookings and orders parts for independent bike shops. Maya Okafor
built it after six years running a shop in Leeds."

**What changed:** Every word in the original could describe any software company. The rewrite
says what it does and the one fact no competitor has.
